Learning Paths

Seventeen pages is a lot of guide. This page tells you which ones to read, in what order, and what to skip — depending on whether you want to build AI systems, work alongside them, or simply understand what everyone is arguing about.

No prerequisites Reading time ~12 min

How This Guide Is Built

Every technical page follows the same shape, so you can calibrate how deeply to read:

  • An "In plain terms" box opens each major idea. If you read only these, you will still come away with an accurate mental model. They assume nothing.
  • The main text explains the mechanism properly, with equations and real numbers. It assumes you are comfortable with programming and school-level mathematics, and it defines everything else.
  • Callouts flag the thing that is easy to get wrong, the surprising consequence, or the number that changes how you think about a topic.
  • "Check yourself" questions appear near the end of most pages. Answer before expanding. If you cannot, reread that section — this is genuinely how the material sticks.
  • Key takeaways close each page. Useful for revision, and useful for deciding whether to read the page at all.

The 🌙 / ☀️ button in the navigation bar switches between light and dark themes and remembers your choice. Every page has an "On this page" outline at the top, and previous / next links at the bottom that follow the recommended reading order.

What Depends on What

The Maths You Actually Need

In plain terms

Far less than people assume. Almost all of deep learning is multiplying grids of numbers together and nudging them in the direction that reduces error. If you understand what a matrix multiplication does and what a derivative means, you can follow every equation on this site.

TopicWhat you needWhere it shows up
Linear algebraVectors, matrices, matrix multiplication, dimensions matching up. Not eigendecompositionsEvery layer of every network. This is the one that genuinely matters
CalculusWhat a derivative is, what a gradient is, the chain rule. You will never compute one by handBackpropagation and every optimiser
ProbabilityDistributions, sampling, expected value, why log-probabilities are used instead of probabilitiesLoss functions, sampling strategies, diffusion
ComplexityBig-O notation, and the instinct to ask what grows with whatWhy attention is quadratic, why context is expensive, why KV caching exists
Numerical representationFloating point: exponent, mantissa, precision versus rangeFP32 / BF16 / FP8 / INT4 — the single most practically useful item on this list
The one concept that unlocks the most If you learn only one thing properly, make it matrix multiplication and dimensional analysis. Nearly every architecture diagram in AI is a claim about shapes: this tensor of shape (batch, sequence, dimension) meets this weight matrix of shape (dimension, dimension) and produces that. Once you can track shapes through a network, transformer diagrams stop being intimidating and start being readable — and you will catch your own bugs before you run anything.

Things to Unlearn

Some widely repeated statements about AI are wrong, or true in a way that misleads. Correcting these early saves a lot of confusion later.

Common claimWhat is actually true
"It's just autocomplete"The training objective is next-token prediction. That says nothing about what internal machinery is required to do it well — and interpretability work finds planning, world representations and abstract features inside. The objective is simple; the solution is not
"It doesn't understand, it just pattern-matches"This distinction is asserted far more often than it is defined. Until "understanding" is specified in a way that can be tested, the claim is not falsifiable — and it does not predict what the system can or cannot do, which is the only thing that matters practically
"Bigger models are always better"Scale was the dominant axis for several years. It is now one of three, alongside post-training and inference-time compute. A smaller model that thinks longer often beats a larger one that answers immediately
"Hallucination is a bug to be fixed"It is a direct consequence of an objective that rewards plausible continuations. It can be reduced substantially — grounding, retrieval, verification, calibration training — but it is a property of the approach, not a defect in the implementation
"The model is learning from our conversation"Weights are fixed at inference. In-context learning happens entirely within the context window and vanishes when the conversation ends. Any persistence is a separate storage system a developer built
"AI will take physical jobs first"The reverse happened. See Robotics and Moravec's paradox — the manual work turned out to be the hard part
"Open source AI"Almost always open weights: parameters without training data, code or reproducibility. A meaningful distinction, discussed on the Economics page
"Benchmark X proves model Y is best"Benchmarks saturate, leak into training data, and are reported under wildly varying protocols. See Evaluation before believing any chart

Projects That Teach

Reading gets you a model of the field; building gets you an intuition for it. These are ordered by effort, and each targets a specific misunderstanding.

Weekend

  • Tokenise text by hand. Run a tokeniser over English, code, and a non-Latin script. Compare token counts. You will never again wonder why some languages cost more.
  • Build a tiny neural network with no framework. Two layers, NumPy, backprop written out. Nothing else makes gradients feel real.
  • Measure your own KV cache. Compute the memory for a 70B model at 100K context. Compare to a GPU's capacity. The whole serving field suddenly makes sense.

Week

  • Train a small character-level transformer on a text you like. Watch loss fall and samples improve. This is the entire pipeline in miniature.
  • Fine-tune with LoRA on a consumer GPU. See how little data changes behaviour — and how easily it overfits.
  • Write an agent loop from scratch. Three tools, no framework, under 200 lines. The mystique evaporates and the real difficulty appears.

Month

  • Build an eval suite for a real task, with a golden set and a calibrated judge. The most transferable skill on this list.
  • Serve a model yourself with a modern inference engine. Measure throughput against batch size and watch the tradeoff curve appear.
  • Reproduce a paper's headline result — and discover how much was in the details rather than the abstract.

Keeping Up Without Drowning

The volume of AI output is unmanageable and getting worse. A few habits that hold up:

  • Prioritise mechanism over news. A model release is obsolete in months; understanding why attention is quadratic is permanent. This whole guide is organised on that principle.
  • Read the method section first. Abstracts oversell. Methods and appendices tell you what was actually done, and limitations sections are where the honesty lives.
  • Always ask for the protocol. "State of the art" without shots, attempts, reasoning budget and scaffolding is marketing, not measurement.
  • Distrust demonstration videos. Especially in robotics and agents. Ask about success rate over many attempts, intervention rate, and how many takes it took.
  • Follow the constraints, not the announcements. Power, memory bandwidth, packaging capacity and data availability predict what happens next far better than any roadmap does.
  • Build something small. One afternoon of building corrects more misconceptions than a month of reading, including misconceptions acquired from this site.
A note on this guide's own limitations Everything here describing the industry — figures, capabilities, company behaviour, regulation — has a shelf life measured in months. The mathematics, the architectures and the physical constraints do not. Where those two categories conflict in your reading, trust the second and go check the first. And treat this guide the way it asks you to treat benchmarks: as a useful, dated, fallible instrument rather than an authority.

Key takeaways

  • Read Neural Networks and LLMs first regardless of route. Everything else is optional and reorderable.
  • Plain-terms boxes carry the ideas; equations carry the precision. Take whichever you need and skip the other without guilt.
  • The maths barrier is lower than advertised — matrix multiplication, the chain rule, and floating-point representation cover nearly all of it.
  • Build something small early. Intuition arrives through implementation, not through reading.
  • Prefer mechanism to news: the constraints outlive every announcement.
