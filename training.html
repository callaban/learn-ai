Training

Training Foundational AI Models

How raw compute, data, and gradient descent produce a model with hundreds of billions of parameters that can reason, generate, and generalise — and why each design choice scales to the hardware limits of a 100,000-GPU cluster.

The Training Objective

In plain terms

Training is guess-and-adjust, repeated an astronomical number of times. Show the model some text with the next word hidden. It guesses. Measure how wrong the guess was. Nudge every one of its billions of internal numbers a tiny amount in the direction that would have made the guess less wrong. Repeat for months. Nobody tells the model about grammar, facts, or reasoning — all of that turns out to be what "predict the next word well" requires, if you do enough of it.

Training is an optimisation problem. Given a dataset 𝒟 = {(xᵢ, yᵢ)} and a model fθ parameterised by weights θ, find θ* that minimises the empirical risk:

θ* = argmin_θ (1/|𝒟|) Σᵢ L(fθ(xᵢ), yᵢ) For language models, xᵢ is a token sequence and yᵢ is the next token. The loss is cross-entropy: L = −log P_θ(yᵢ | xᵢ)

The loss landscape is a high-dimensional surface — in a 70B-parameter model, a point in a 70-billion-dimensional space. Gradient descent navigates this surface by repeatedly moving in the direction of steepest descent.

Optimisers: From SGD to Adam

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)

Rather than computing the gradient over the full dataset (prohibitively expensive), SGD estimates it from a random mini-batch B ⊂ 𝒟 of size m:

g = (1/m) Σᵢ∈B ∇_θ L(fθ(xᵢ), yᵢ) θ ← θ − η · g η = learning rate (e.g. 3×10⁻⁴ for large models)

SGD with momentum adds a velocity term that accumulates past gradients, smoothing oscillations in the loss surface:

v ← μv + g (μ = momentum coefficient, typically 0.9) θ ← θ − η · v

Adam: Adaptive Moment Estimation

Adam, introduced by Kingma & Ba (2014), is the dominant optimiser for large model training. It maintains per-parameter first and second moment estimates:

m_t = β₁ m_{t-1} + (1 − β₁) g_t [first moment: mean of gradient] v_t = β₂ v_{t-1} + (1 − β₂) g_t² [second moment: uncentered variance] Bias correction (moments start at 0, biased toward 0 at start): m̂_t = m_t / (1 − β₁ᵗ) v̂_t = v_t / (1 − β₂ᵗ) θ_t = θ_{t-1} − η · m̂_t / (√v̂_t + ε) Hyperparameters: β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95, ε=10⁻⁸, η=1–3×10⁻⁴

The denominator √v̂_t normalises the update by the historical root-mean-square of the gradient. Parameters with large, consistent gradients receive small steps; rarely-updated parameters with small gradients receive large steps. This adaptive learning rate is critical for models with diverse parameter types (embeddings, attention weights, layer norms) that have vastly different gradient scales.

Memory cost of Adam Adam stores two moment vectors (m and v) in addition to the parameters (θ) and gradients (g). For a 70B parameter model in float32, that is 4 × 70B × 4 bytes ≈ 1.1 TB of optimizer state alone. This is why distributed training uses sharded optimizer states (ZeRO Stage 2/3 or FSDP).

Learning Rate Scheduling

A fixed learning rate is rarely optimal. Modern LLM training uses a two-phase schedule:

Phase 1: Linear warm-up over W steps η(t) = η_max · t/W for t ≤ W Phase 2: Cosine decay from step W to total steps T η(t) = η_min + 0.5(η_max − η_min)(1 + cos(π(t−W)/(T−W))) Typical: η_max=3e-4, η_min=3e-5, W=2000 steps, T=1M steps

Warm-up prevents large initial updates before moment estimates stabilise. Cosine decay is empirically superior to step decay because it smoothly reduces the learning rate, allowing fine-grained fitting near convergence.

Data: Scale, Quality, and Preprocessing

A foundational model's capability is bounded by the quality and diversity of its training data. GPT-3 was trained on 300B tokens; LLaMA 3 on 15 trillion tokens. The primary corpus for language models is the web — Common Crawl alone is hundreds of terabytes of compressed text.

Data Pipeline

  1. Crawl and download — Raw HTML from web crawls, books, code repositories, academic papers, Wikipedia. Raw Common Crawl dumps are ~80 TB/year.
  2. Extraction and cleaning — Strip HTML, deduplicate near-identical documents using MinHash LSH, remove boilerplate (menus, footers), filter low-quality pages using a classifier trained on high-quality reference data.
  3. Deduplication — Exact and fuzzy deduplication dramatically improves model quality. A document seen 100× during training receives 100× the gradient signal — the model memorises it rather than generalising from it.
  4. Tokenisation — Text is split into subword tokens using Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or SentencePiece. The vocabulary size V is typically 32,000–130,000. The average English word tokenises to 1.3–1.5 tokens.
  5. Packing — Individual documents are concatenated and split into fixed-length context windows (e.g. 4,096 or 8,192 tokens), with a special end-of-document token at boundaries. GPUs require fixed-size inputs; this avoids padding waste.
  6. Shuffling — Documents are shuffled globally so each mini-batch contains a diverse mix of sources, preventing the model from learning batch-level correlations.

Tokenisation in Detail

BPE starts from a character-level vocabulary and iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols. After 32,000 merges, common words are single tokens, rare words are decomposed into familiar subwords. The model never encounters an unknown token — any byte sequence is representable.

Example BPE tokenisation (GPT-4 tokeniser): "Backpropagation" → ["Back", "prop", "ag", "ation"] "CUDA" → ["CUD", "A"] "NVLink" → ["NV", "Link"]

Mixed-Precision Training

Full float32 (FP32) requires 4 bytes per parameter. For a 70B parameter model, parameters alone consume 280 GB. Modern training uses mixed precision to reduce memory and accelerate compute on tensor cores:

FormatBitsRangeUse case
FP3232±3.4×10³⁸Master weights, gradient accumulation, loss scaling
BF1616±3.4×10³⁸Forward/backward activations, gradient computation (preferred)
FP1616±65,504Activations — requires loss scaling to prevent underflow
FP8 (E4M3/E5M2)8variesForward pass only in Hopper/Ada GPUs; 2× throughput

The standard AMP (Automatic Mixed Precision) pattern: maintain a master copy of weights in FP32, perform forward/backward in BF16, cast gradients back to FP32 for the optimizer step, then cast updated weights back to BF16 for the next forward pass. BF16 shares its exponent range with FP32 (8-bit exponent), so overflow is rare — unlike FP16 which requires manual loss scaling to prevent underflow of small gradients.

Memory per parameter (70B model): BF16 weights : 70B × 2B = 140 GB FP32 master : 70B × 4B = 280 GB FP32 gradients : 70B × 4B = 280 GB Adam m, v : 70B × 4B × 2 = 560 GB ───────────────────────────────────── Total optimizer : ~1,260 GB (across all GPUs) With ZeRO-3 sharding across 512 GPUs: ~2.5 GB/GPU

Distributed Training

A single GPU cannot hold a 70B-parameter model in training mode. Modern training distributes across thousands of GPUs using three parallelism strategies, often combined (3D parallelism):

Data Parallelism

Each GPU holds a full copy of the model. Different GPUs process different batches. After each backward pass, gradients are averaged across all GPUs with an all-reduce collective operation before updating weights. Scales to arbitrary batch sizes; requires each GPU to hold the full model — infeasible for very large models.

Tensor Parallelism (Megatron-style)

Individual weight matrices are sharded across GPUs. A matrix multiplication W × x is split into column-wise or row-wise shards, computed in parallel, and results combined with all-reduce or all-gather. For a transformer attention layer:

Q, K, V projection weights split column-wise across P GPUs. GPU i holds W_i ∈ ℝ^{d×(d/P)} Each GPU computes Q_i = x W_i, K_i = x W_i, V_i = x W_i Results are gathered and attention computed across heads.

Pipeline Parallelism

The model is partitioned into L sequential stages across groups of GPUs. Stage 0 processes layer 0–15, stage 1 processes layers 16–31, etc. Micro-batches pipeline through stages, keeping all GPUs busy. Introduces a pipeline bubble (idle time at start/end of a batch) — minimised by using many micro-batches per macro-batch (1F1B schedule).

ZeRO Optimizer (Zero Redundancy Optimizer)

Microsoft's ZeRO partitions optimizer state, gradients, and parameters across data-parallel ranks, eliminating redundancy:

ZeRO Stage 1: Partition optimizer state → 4× memory reduction ZeRO Stage 2: + Partition gradients → 8× memory reduction ZeRO Stage 3: + Partition parameters → 16× memory reduction (for data-parallel degree 16) At 1024-GPU data parallelism with ZeRO-3: Memory per GPU ≈ Total_training_memory / 1024

Scaling Laws

The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., DeepMind, 2022) empirically determined the optimal relationship between model size P, training tokens D, and compute budget C:

Optimal: D ≈ 20 × P If C = 6PD FLOPs (6 for fwd+bwd+update) Then: C = 6P(20P) = 120P² → P_opt = √(C/120) → D_opt = 20 × √(C/120) Example: C = 10²³ FLOPs P_opt ≈ 9.1B parameters, D_opt ≈ 182B tokens Loss as function of P and D (power law): L(P,D) = E + A/P^α + B/D^β E ≈ 1.69 nats (irreducible entropy of natural language) A ≈ 406.4, B ≈ 410.7, α ≈ 0.34, β ≈ 0.28

The key finding: prior work (GPT-3 etc.) dramatically undertrained large models. A 70B model trained on 1T tokens is suboptimal compared to a 35B model trained on 2T tokens at the same compute budget — the latter achieves lower loss. This led to LLaMA and Mistral's strategy of training smaller models longer, producing models that are cheaper to run while achieving comparable quality.

Practical implication Training compute is paid once. Inference compute is paid for every query, forever. The Chinchilla insight justifies training a model 2–4× longer than naive scaling would suggest, if the model will be served millions of times. Smaller, well-trained models are cheaper to deploy.

How the Picture Has Changed Since Chinchilla

In plain terms

Chinchilla answered the question "given a fixed training budget, what is the best model I can train?" That was the right question when training dominated costs. It is no longer the only question, because three things happened: serving costs came to dominate, high-quality text started running out, and it turned out you could buy capability at inference time instead.

  • Deliberate overtraining. Chinchilla optimises loss per unit of training compute. If a model will serve trillions of tokens, the right objective is total lifetime cost, which favours a smaller model trained far past the Chinchilla point. Modern flagship models are routinely trained on token-to-parameter ratios many times the Chinchilla ratio — the returns diminish, but they do not vanish, and the inference saving is permanent.
  • The data wall. Chinchilla assumes tokens are freely available. At 15–20 trillion tokens, a training run has consumed a large fraction of the high-quality text that exists. The responses are all imperfect: multiple epochs over the same data (which works better than theory predicted, but saturates), aggressive filtering and reweighting toward higher-quality sources, non-text modalities as additional signal, and synthetic data.
  • Synthetic data, carefully. Using a strong model to generate training data for another model works well when the output can be verified — solutions checked against a proof or a test suite, or reasoning traces filtered by whether they reached the right answer. It degrades when generated text is recycled unfiltered, since errors and stylistic tics compound across generations. The distinction between verified and unverified synthetic data is the whole ballgame.
  • Two more scaling axes. Post-training reinforcement learning and inference-time reasoning both convert compute into capability without touching pretraining. Frontier labs now allocate a substantial fraction of total compute to post-training — a reallocation that would have looked bizarre in 2022. See Reasoning Models.
Scaling laws are empirical, not physical Power-law fits are extrapolations from observed data, and their constants shift with architecture, data mixture and tokeniser. They have held remarkably well over several orders of magnitude, which is why the industry bet so heavily on them. But "the curve keeps going" is a forecast, not a law, and the curve says nothing about which capabilities a given loss reduction buys. Loss is smooth; usefulness is not.

The Training Loop

Pretraining, Fine-Tuning, and RLHF

Pretraining: Next-Token Prediction

A foundation model is pretrained on a massive, diverse text corpus with the causal language modelling objective: predict the next token given all preceding tokens. This is self-supervised — no human labels needed. The model implicitly learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and world knowledge to minimise prediction loss.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The pretrained model is then fine-tuned on a curated dataset of (instruction, response) pairs written or curated by humans. SFT teaches the model the format of helpful responses and aligns output style with human expectations. Typically involves 10,000–100,000 high-quality examples, trained for 1–3 epochs at a lower learning rate (~5×10⁻⁶).

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

  1. Collect preference data: For a given prompt, sample K responses from the SFT model. Human raters rank the responses (e.g. preferred / not preferred).
  2. Train a reward model: A separate neural network r_φ(prompt, response) → scalar is trained to predict human preference rankings. Loss: r(preferred) > r(not preferred).
  3. Optimise with PPO: Use Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) to update the language model (policy) to maximise expected reward, with a KL-divergence penalty to prevent the model from deviating too far from the SFT initialisation:
    Objective = E[r_φ(x, y)] − β · KL[π_θ(y|x) ‖ π_SFT(y|x)] β controls the tradeoff between reward maximisation and staying close to the SFT policy. Too small β → reward hacking (model finds adversarial inputs that fool the reward model). Too large β → model barely changes from SFT.

Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), introduced in 2023, sidesteps the reward model entirely and directly optimises the language model on preference data using a closed-form loss derived from the Bradley-Terry preference model. DPO is simpler to implement and, for many alignment tasks, has displaced PPO.

Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards

In plain terms

RLHF asks humans which of two answers they prefer. That works until the questions get harder than the humans. The alternative is to train only on problems where a computer can check the answer — does the code compile and pass its tests, does the proof verify, is the arithmetic right — and let the model attempt them millions of times. No human is in the loop, so the process scales with compute instead of with labelling hours. This is what produced reasoning models, and it is the most important change in training since RLHF itself.

The RLVR loop 1. Sample G attempts at a problem from the current policy 2. Score each attempt with a program, not a person: tests pass / fail · proof checks / does not · answer matches / does not 3. Compute each attempt's advantage relative to the group's mean score 4. Update the policy toward high-advantage attempts, with a KL penalty holding it near the reference model 5. Repeat, on harder problems, for a very long time GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimisation) is the widely used variant. Its practical advantage over PPO: by using the group mean as the baseline, it eliminates the separately trained value network — removing a large model from the training loop and roughly halving memory and complexity. What emerges, unprompted, from this objective: · the model writes out longer and longer reasoning as training proceeds · it starts verifying its own intermediate steps · it backtracks after dead ends instead of committing to a bad path None of this was demonstrated to it. It is what the optimisation found.
The limits of verifiable rewards RLVR requires an automatic grader, which restricts it to domains with checkable answers — mathematics, code, formal logic, structured tool use. Capability gains transfer partially to unverifiable domains such as writing and analysis, but only partially, and the transfer is not well understood. There is also a persistent hazard: the model optimises the checker, not the goal. If the grader can be satisfied by special-casing its inputs, weakening a test, or exploiting a bug in the environment, a sufficiently capable optimiser will find that route. Reward hacking is not an occasional bug in RLVR; it is the default behaviour that careful environment design exists to prevent.

Two observations about this table are worth more than the table itself. First, post-training is where models are differentiated now: pretraining recipes have converged considerably across labs, while post-training is where the proprietary craft lives. Second, distillation is why capability diffuses so fast. Once a capability exists in any model, a smaller model can be trained to imitate it far more cheaply than it was originally created — which is a large part of why the gap between frontier and open-weight models is measured in months rather than years, and a large part of the commoditisation argument on the Economics page.

Check yourself

Why does the industry train models well past the Chinchilla-optimal point?

Chinchilla minimises loss for a fixed training budget. It ignores serving cost, which is paid on every query forever. A smaller model trained longer costs more to train and much less to run, so lifetime cost favours overtraining whenever the model will be served at scale.

What does GRPO remove from PPO, and why does that matter practically?

The separately trained value network. GRPO uses the mean score of a group of sampled attempts as the baseline instead, which removes an entire large model from the training loop — roughly halving memory and a good deal of engineering complexity.

Why is verified synthetic data safe when unverified synthetic data degrades models?

Verification filters out errors before they enter training. Unverified generated text recycles the teacher's mistakes and stylistic quirks, and those compound across generations — the model learns its predecessor's flaws as if they were features of the language.

Key takeaways

  • Training is gradient descent on next-token prediction. Everything a model knows is a side effect of getting good at that one objective.
  • Adam's per-parameter adaptive step sizes are what make training stable at this scale, at the cost of two extra optimiser states per parameter.
  • Distributed training combines data, tensor, pipeline and expert parallelism; which combination you use is dictated by memory and interconnect bandwidth, not preference.
  • Chinchilla answers "best model for a training budget." Deliberate overtraining answers "cheapest model over its serving life," and that is now usually the right question.
  • Post-training — SFT, preference optimisation, and reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards — is where frontier models are differentiated, and where reasoning ability comes from.
  • Reward hacking is the default failure mode of any automatic grader. Environment design is safety work.
